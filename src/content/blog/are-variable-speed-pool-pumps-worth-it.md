If your pump has failed or you're planning ahead for when it will, you'll run into this decision: stick with a standard single-speed pump, or spend more upfront on a variable-speed model. Here's what actually differs between them.

## The core difference

A single-speed pump runs at one speed — full power, whenever it's on. A variable-speed pump can run at a range of speeds, from a slow trickle for regular filtration up to full speed for tasks that need it, like vacuuming or running a water feature. That flexibility is where all the other differences come from.

## Where variable-speed pumps generally win

- **Lower energy use for everyday filtration.** Most of a pump's job is just circulating and filtering water for hours a day, which doesn't need full power. Running at a lower speed for longer draws meaningfully less electricity than running at full speed for a shorter period, and pump energy use scales steeply with speed — even a modest reduction in speed can produce a large reduction in power draw.
- **Quieter operation.** At lower speeds, variable-speed pumps run noticeably quieter than a single-speed pump at full blast, which matters if your equipment pad is anywhere near a patio, bedroom window, or a neighbor's fence line.
- **Less mechanical wear over time.** Running at a lower speed most of the time generally puts less strain on the motor than constant full-speed operation, which can extend the pump's usable life.
- **More control.** You can dial in exactly how much flow different tasks need instead of everything running at the same single setting.

## Where single-speed pumps still make sense

- **Lower upfront cost.** Variable-speed pumps cost more to buy and install, and that difference isn't trivial.
- **Simplicity.** A single-speed pump is a simpler piece of equipment with less programming and fewer settings to manage.
- **Payback period depends on usage.** If your pump only runs a few hours a day, or electricity costs where you live are low, the energy savings take longer to offset the higher upfront cost.

## How to actually decide

The math comes down to how many hours a day your pump runs and your local electricity rates. If you're running filtration for long stretches each day (common for larger pools or pools that need extended circulation for chemical distribution), a variable-speed pump's energy savings tend to add up meaningfully over its lifespan. If your pump's runtime is minimal, the upfront cost difference matters more relative to what you'd save.

The other consideration: if your existing pump is failing and needs replacement anyway, the incremental cost of upgrading to variable-speed at that point is smaller than the full cost of a standalone upgrade — which is often when it makes the most sense to make the switch.
